Duck Confit - French Slow-Cooked Duck

Why This Recipe

Peak duck season in the UK makes this the classic dish to crack this week. It's a genuinely new technique (dry-cure, then 8 hours in gentle duck fat) and the entire cook is hands-off oven time - the exact low-effort style you've been loving, but with a restaurant-quality, fall-apart tender result.

Ingredients

  • 4 duck legs, skin on, bone in (Marylands), about 250g / 8oz each
  • 750ml / 25oz duck fat
  • 4 tsp kosher or cooking salt (for the dry cure)
  • 1/3 cup thyme sprigs, lightly packed
  • 1/3 cup sage sprigs, lightly packed
  • 1/3 cup rosemary sprigs, lightly packed
  • 1/3 cup oregano sprigs, lightly packed
  • 6 garlic cloves, smashed, skin on
  • 1 shallot, sliced 1/3 inch / 7mm thick
  • 1 tbsp black peppercorns
  • 1 tbsp coriander seeds
  • 8 star anise
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 2 tbsp juniper berries
  • 8 whole cloves

Instructions

Prep - Dry Cure

  • Combine the duck legs with all the dry-cure ingredients (salt, thyme, sage, rosemary, oregano, garlic, shallot, peppercorns, coriander seeds, star anise, bay leaves, juniper berries and cloves) in a large bowl. Toss well with your hands until every leg is well coated in the spice mix.
  • Transfer to a ceramic or glass dish just large enough for the legs to sit snugly in a single layer. Cover and refrigerate for 12 to 24 hours (12 hours is plenty if you're in a hurry - don't go much longer).
  • Remove the legs from the fridge, brush off the loose cure, and rinse under cold running water to wash away all the salt and dark spice bits. Pat completely dry with paper towels.

Cook - Slow Confit

  • Preheat the oven to 100C / 210F (80C fan) with a shelf in the middle.
  • Place the rinsed, dried duck legs skin-side up in a small metal roasting pan with high sides - they should sit snugly, touching, but in a single layer.
  • Pour the duck fat over the legs. It should come up to almost cover them.
  • Set the pan on the hob over medium-high heat and heat the fat until it is bubbling energetically across the whole surface (not just around the edges). This seals the duck in its fat.
  • Turn off the hob. Lay a sheet of baking / parchment paper over the surface of the fat, pushed gently to remove air bubbles, then seal the pan tightly with a double layer of foil.
  • Carefully transfer the sealed pan to the oven and slow-cook for 8 hours.

Finish - Crisp the Skin

  • While the confit cooks, plan the final step. When ready, remove one leg and turn it over: the flesh should be fall-apart tender and the bone should pull free easily with gentle pressure on the joint. If it's still firm, cook a little longer.
  • Preheat the oven to 240C / 465F (220C fan). Set a rack over a deep roasting pan.
  • Take the duck out of the fat, let the excess drip off, and pat dry. Pour about 1 inch / 2.5cm of boiling water into the roasting pan (this catches the dripping fat so it doesn't smoke). Place the legs skin-side up on the rack.
  • Roast for 40 minutes, turning the pan as needed, until the skin is deep golden and shatteringly crisp.
  • Rest the legs briefly (2-3 minutes) before serving.

Serve

  • Serve the crisp-skinned confit with a classic French side - the original pairs it with lentil ragout, but it's also brilliant with roast potatoes cooked in the confit fat, a green salad, or crusty bread for the juices.
  • To save the legs for later: strain off the juices, lower the legs into a non-reactive jar, pour over the cooled (now solidified) confit fat to seal them, then refrigerate up to 5 days or freeze up to 3 months. To use, melt the fat gently and go straight to the crisping step.

Pro Tips

  • Duck fat is the soul of a proper confit. If you can't find it, a 50/50 blend of lard and good olive oil works in a pinch, but it's worth ordering a bottle online - the leftover confit fat keeps for weeks and is gold for roast potatoes or pan-frying.
  • The 100C / 210F low-and-slow cook is what gives the meat its fall-apart texture - resist cranking the heat up to speed it through, that's what makes confit different from a braise. The 1 inch of water in the pan at the end is there to stop the dripping fat from smoking, not to steam the bird.
