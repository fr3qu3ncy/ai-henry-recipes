Kare-Kare (Filipino Oxtail Peanut Stew)

Why This Recipe

Kare-kare is the centrepiece of the Filipino fiesta table and a completely new cuisine for you — oxtail braised fall-apart tender in a thick, nutty peanut sauce coloured deep gold with annatto. The twist that makes it brilliant: the sauce is deliberately mild and a touch sweet, and it's always served with a spoonful of sautéed bagoong (fermented shrimp paste) on the side for a salty-funky punch. A long, hands-off braise that deepens overnight and keeps for days.

Ingredients

  • 1.2 kg oxtail, cut into 5-6 cm pieces
  • 300 g beef tripe, cleaned (optional but classic)
  • 1 large onion, quartered (for the braise)
  • 6 cloves garlic, crushed (for the braise)
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 tsp whole black peppercorns
  • 2 tsp salt
  • 10 cups water, or enough to cover
  • 3/4 cup natural creamy peanut butter (unsweetened)
  • 1/2 cup finely ground roasted peanuts
  • 3 tbsp toasted rice powder (toasted rice flour)
  • 1 cup annatto water (steep 3 tbsp annatto/achuete seeds in 1 cup warm water, strain)
  • 4 cups reserved braising stock, divided, plus extra to thin
  • 1 medium onion, finely chopped (for the sauce)
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced (for the sauce)
  • 2-3 tbsp cooking oil
  • 200 g sitaw (yard-long beans), cut into 5 cm pieces
  • 1 medium eggplant, sliced thick on the bias
  • 1 small banana blossom (puso), sliced and soaked in salted water (optional)
  • 1 bunch pechay or baby bok choy, separated
  • 1/2 cup sautéed bagoong alamang (fermented shrimp paste), for serving
  • Chopped fresh chilli, to mix into the bagoong (optional)

Instructions

Prep

  • Steep the annatto (achuete) seeds in 1 cup warm water, then strain and set the gold-tinted liquid aside — it colours the sauce.
  • Toast your rice powder: dry-toast raw rice in a pan until golden and aromatic, grind fine, and set aside (or buy pre-made toasted rice powder). Toasting deepens the nutty flavour and helps the sauce thicken smoothly.
  • Sauté the bagoong alamang in a little oil with the chopped chilli until fragrant — this is your salty condiment, served on the side.
  • Cut the oxtail (and tripe, if using) into 5-6 cm pieces and soak the banana blossom in salted water if you're using it.

Cook

  • Put the oxtail and tripe in a large pot with the quartered onion, crushed garlic, bay leaves, peppercorns, salt and enough water to cover. Bring to a boil, skim off the scum, then cover and simmer gently for 2-3 hours (or high-pressure for 45-60 minutes with a 15-minute natural release), topping up with hot water, until fork-tender.
  • Lift the meat onto a covered dish to keep warm. Strain the stock, skim off excess fat, and measure out 4 cups for the sauce, holding extra back to thin if needed.
  • Meanwhile, blanch the vegetables in separate batches in boiling water until tender: sitaw 3-5 minutes, eggplant 4-6 minutes, thin banana blossom 8-12 minutes, and pechay 1-2 minutes. Drain — texture, not the timer, is the cue.

Build the Sauce

  • Heat the sauce oil in a wide pan. Soften the finely chopped onion for 3-4 minutes, add the minced garlic for 30 seconds.
  • Stir in the strained annatto water and 3 cups of the reserved stock; bring to a gentle simmer.
  • Whisk the peanut butter, ground roasted peanuts and toasted rice powder with the remaining 1 cup hot stock until smooth, then whisk it into the pan.
  • Simmer gently for 10-15 minutes, stirring along the bottom, until the rice powder is cooked and the sauce is smooth and coats a spoon.

Finish

  • Return the oxtail and tripe to the sauce and simmer 5-10 minutes until hot throughout.
  • Adjust with extra stock to a thick-but-pourable consistency — it should coat the meat, not set around it.
  • Add the drained vegetables just long enough to reheat, or arrange them beside the stew.
  • Taste before adding any more salt — remember the bagoong you're serving alongside is salty.

Serve

  • Serve hot over steamed white rice with the sautéed bagoong on the side, stirred into each bite. The sauce base can be made a day ahead — the flavour deepens overnight.

Pro Tips

  • Use natural, unsweetened creamy peanut butter — commercial peanut butter with added sugar throws off the whole balance and makes the sauce cloying.
  • The bagoong on the side is non-negotiable. The peanut sauce is deliberately mild and a touch sweet; a spoonful of the fermented shrimp paste per bite is what gives kare-kare its signature salty-umami punch. Skipping it means missing half the dish.
